# Part 021 — Working with Product Owner on Complex Domains

## 1. Source notes and scope boundary

This part uses public Scrum guidance and general enterprise product engineering practice.

Public references to verify independently:

- The Scrum Guide states that the Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.
- The Scrum Guide also states that the Product Owner is accountable for effective Product Backlog management, including Product Goal communication, PBI clarity, ordering, and transparency.
- Scrum does not say senior engineers own product value. Senior engineers contribute technical, operational, and domain-risk insight so the Product Owner can make better product decisions.
- CSG internal Product Owner authority, roadmap process, customer escalation process, product management structure, and release decision rights must be verified internally.

This part does **not** assume the exact Product Owner structure at CSG, whether the team uses one PO per team, a product manager plus PO model, proxy PO, product owner group, business analyst role, or scaled agile product layer.

Use the internal verification checklist before applying any recommendation literally.

---

## 2. Core thesis

A senior engineer should not try to become the Product Owner.

A senior engineer should help the Product Owner make better product decisions by making technical reality visible.

In complex enterprise domains, the Product Owner often has to balance:

- customer value,
- roadmap commitments,
- commercial urgency,
- support escalations,
- release constraints,
- integration dependencies,
- regulatory/compliance expectations,
- technical debt,
- production incidents,
- architecture risk.

The senior engineer's role is to translate hidden technical risk into backlog-visible product risk.

Weak collaboration:

> The PO writes tickets. Engineers implement them.

Stronger collaboration:

> The PO owns product value and backlog ordering. Engineers expose feasibility, risk, dependency, quality, operability, and technical trade-offs early enough that product decisions are informed.

In CSG Quote & Order style systems, this collaboration is critical because a seemingly simple product request may affect:

- product catalog versioning,
- pricing behavior,
- quote lifecycle,
- approval authority,
- order conversion,
- order decomposition,
- fulfillment integration,
- billing handoff,
- Kafka events,
- PostgreSQL migrations,
- audit evidence,
- tenant-specific configuration,
- cloud/on-prem deployment,
- customer integration contracts.

The PO cannot safely order the backlog if these risks stay invisible.

## 3. The Product Owner accountability from an engineer's perspective

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing product value.

For engineers, that means the PO needs information about value and risk.

Engineering should help with risk and feasibility, not replace product judgment.

| Product Owner concern | Senior engineer contribution |
|---|---|
| What should we build next? | Expose technical dependency, feasibility, and hidden risk. |
| Which PBI has highest value? | Explain operational/customer impact of technical work. |
| Is this story ready? | Clarify unknowns, missing acceptance criteria, and testability. |
| Can this fit in a Sprint? | Help slice behavior and estimate uncertainty. |
| Can we release this? | Explain Definition of Done, rollout, migration, and observability needs. |
| What should we defer? | Explain cost of delay vs cost of poor quality. |
| What customer problem are we solving? | Connect implementation details to customer journey and support evidence. |

Good PO-engineer collaboration is not role confusion.

It is role complementarity.

## 4. The failure mode: ticket factory Scrum

Ticket factory Scrum happens when Product Owner and engineers interact only through tickets.

Symptoms:

- PBIs describe implementation tasks, not product outcomes.
- Engineers receive tickets late and discover risk during coding.
- Refinement is mostly estimation.
- Acceptance criteria are shallow.
- Technical work is hidden under feature work.
- Bugs create blame because assumptions were not shared.
- Sprint Review demos UI but not production readiness.
- Technical debt never competes fairly with features.
- Sprint Planning becomes capacity filling.

Example ticket-factory PBI:

```md
As a user, I want order cancellation so that I can cancel orders.
```

This is not enough for Quote & Order.

A senior engineer should help expose questions:

- Which order states are cancellable?
- Is cancellation full or item-level?
- What happens if fulfillment already started?
- Does cancellation send event to downstream fulfillment?
- Does it affect billing?
- Does it require customer authorization?
- Does it create audit event?
- Is cancellation idempotent?
- Is cancellation exposed through TMF-aligned API?
- How does support recover partial cancellation failure?

The PO owns the product decision, but engineering must make these questions visible.

## 5. Working agreement with Product Owner

A senior engineer should establish a practical collaboration agreement with the PO.

### 5.1 Suggested working agreement

```md
# PO–Engineering Working Agreement

## Product Owner provides
- Product goal and customer/business context.
- Desired outcome and user/customer problem.
- Ordering rationale.
- Stakeholder/customer constraints.
- Acceptance intent.
- Priority trade-off when scope changes.

## Engineering provides
- Feasibility and uncertainty assessment.
- Technical dependency map.
- Risk and failure-mode analysis.
- Slicing options.
- Production readiness implications.
- Estimate confidence.
- Quality and operability requirements.

## Shared responsibility
- Clear PBIs.
- Good Sprint Goal.
- Testable acceptance criteria.
- Increment that satisfies Definition of Done.
- Transparent trade-offs.
```

### 5.2 Why this matters

Without a working agreement:

- PO may assume engineering owns all technical discovery.
- Engineers may assume PO owns all ambiguity.
- QA may receive unclear acceptance criteria.
- Scrum Master may see symptoms but not root cause.
- Team may miss Sprint Goal because risk emerged too late.

A working agreement does not need to be formal bureaucracy.

It can be a 30-minute conversation plus a shared page.

## 6. Product questions senior engineers should ask

Senior engineers should ask product questions that reveal domain intent.

### 6.1 Customer and value questions

Ask:

- Who experiences the problem?
- Is this for sales user, approver, operations user, customer admin, support, or integration client?
- What customer outcome changes if this ships?
- Is this required for one customer, one segment, or the product broadly?
- Is this revenue protection, revenue growth, cost reduction, compliance, customer satisfaction, or operational efficiency?
- What happens if we do nothing this quarter?
- What is the cost of delay?

### 6.2 Domain semantics questions

Ask:

- What does this status mean in business language?
- Is this action legal in every lifecycle state?
- Does this behavior apply to quote, quote item, order, order item, or fulfillment task?
- Is this customer-specific or product-wide?
- Is this behavior aligned with TMF vocabulary or an internal extension?
- Is this a new business capability or a correction of existing behavior?

### 6.3 Acceptance questions

Ask:

- How will the PO know this is correct?
- Which examples prove the behavior?
- Which edge cases must be accepted or rejected?
- What error should the user/integration receive?
- What should be visible in audit/history?
- What should support see when something fails?
- Does this require report/export behavior?

### 6.4 Production questions

Ask:

- Can this be released behind a feature flag?
- Does this change existing customer behavior?
- Does this require data migration or backfill?
- Does this affect in-flight quotes/orders?
- Does this require dashboard or alert changes?
- What is the rollback/disable plan?
- Does support need a runbook update?

These are not attempts to slow delivery.

They are attempts to prevent expensive rework.

## 7. Translating ambiguity into backlog clarity

A Product Owner may start with ambiguous intent.

Example:

> Customers need better order cancellation.

A senior engineer helps refine it into structured backlog knowledge.

### 7.1 Ambiguity breakdown

| Ambiguity | Clarifying question |
|---|---|
| Better for whom? | Sales user, operations user, customer self-service, integration client? |
| Which order type? | New sale, amendment, cancellation, service order, product order? |
| Which state? | Captured, acknowledged, in fulfillment, partially fulfilled, completed? |
| What result? | Cancel request accepted, fulfillment stopped, billing reversed, UI status updated? |
| What exception? | Downstream rejects cancellation, partial fulfillment, duplicate request? |
| What evidence? | Audit event, order history, Kafka event, support note? |

### 7.2 Refinement output

Instead of one vague epic, produce slices:

1. Cancel product order before fulfillment starts.
2. Reject cancellation when order is already completed.
3. Expose cancellation reason and audit history.
4. Emit order cancellation requested event.
5. Handle downstream cancellation accepted callback.
6. Handle downstream cancellation rejected callback.
7. Add support dashboard for cancellation stuck state.

This lets the Product Owner decide ordering with clearer risk and value.

## 8. Product value vs engineering risk

Senior engineers should help PO see that not all engineering work is invisible cost.

Some technical work directly protects product value.

### 8.1 Value translation table

| Engineering concern | Product translation |
|---|---|
| Idempotency for quote acceptance | Prevents duplicate orders after retry/timeouts. |
| Immutable price snapshot | Protects accepted commercial terms and customer trust. |
| Approval audit evidence | Protects governance and dispute resolution. |
| Kafka schema compatibility | Prevents downstream integration breakage. |
| Migration dry run | Reduces upgrade failure risk. |
| Tenant isolation tests | Prevents customer data exposure. |
| Observability dashboard | Detects order failures before customer complaint. |
| Runbook | Reduces incident duration and support escalation cost. |

A senior engineer should phrase technical backlog items this way.

Bad:

> Refactor outbox relay.

Better:

> Reduce risk of missing fulfillment events by making outbox relay idempotent and observable; this protects quote-to-order completion and reduces stuck order incidents.

## 9. Helping PO with backlog ordering

The PO owns ordering, but engineers can provide ordering inputs.

Useful ordering inputs:

- dependency,
- risk,
- uncertainty,
- reversibility,
- customer impact,
- production impact,
- compliance/security exposure,
- incident frequency,
- cost of delay,
- opportunity for learning,
- architecture runway.

### 9.1 Backlog ordering conversation template

```md
## PBI
<name>

## Product value
What customer/business outcome does this enable?

## Technical dependency
What must exist before this is safe/effective?

## Risk if done late
What gets worse if we delay?

## Risk if done early
What cost/complexity do we take now?

## Reversibility
Can we roll this back or disable it?

## Suggested ordering
Before/after which items, and why?
```

### 9.2 Example

PBI: Add advanced discount approval rule.

Engineering input:

- Must clarify approval invalidation before implementation.
- Needs pricing snapshot consistency.
- Needs audit event for approval authority.
- Should not ship before quote acceptance idempotency if acceptance creates order.
- Could be released behind customer-specific flag.

PO decision remains PO decision.

Engineering provides the risk map.

## 10. Helping PO split customer commitments

Enterprise customer commitments often arrive as large scope.

Example:

> Customer needs TMF-compliant product ordering integration.

A senior engineer helps split into safe increments.

### 10.1 Possible slicing path

1. Document supported TMF622 subset and extension model.
2. Expose product order create endpoint for one simple order type.
3. Add product order retrieve endpoint with stable status mapping.
4. Add idempotency and duplicate request handling.
5. Add event/callback notification for order state change.
6. Add cancellation subset.
7. Add amendment subset.
8. Add conformance/regression examples.
9. Add customer-specific extension only after base behavior stable.

This enables the PO to negotiate incremental delivery.

### 10.2 What senior engineers should avoid

Avoid saying:

> This is too big.

Say:

> This is too big to deliver safely as one increment. Here are three slices that each produce testable value and reduce integration risk.

## 11. Product Owner and technical debt

Technical debt needs product conversation, not engineering complaint.

A PO may not prioritize debt if the value is unclear.

The engineer must show the product consequence.

### 11.1 Technical debt framing

Weak:

> The code is messy.

Strong:

> Quote approval logic is duplicated in three places. That increases risk that future pricing changes update one path but not another. The likely customer-visible failure is approval bypass or unnecessary approval. I recommend a small debt item to centralize approval eligibility and add tests before the next discount-rule feature.

### 11.2 Debt prioritization with PO

Ask:

- Does this debt block roadmap work?
- Does it increase incident risk?
- Does it slow every change in this area?
- Does it threaten customer commitments?
- Does it create compliance/security risk?
- Can repayment be sliced?
- What feature benefits from paying it now?

Debt becomes backlog-visible when it has product impact.

## 12. Product Owner and Definition of Done

The PO may accept product behavior, but Definition of Done defines quality for the Increment.

A senior engineer should help keep these separate.

### 12.1 Acceptance criteria vs Definition of Done

| Acceptance criteria | Definition of Done |
|---|---|
| Specific to a PBI | Applies broadly to increments |
| Product behavior and scenario | Quality bar and releasability |
| Owned by product conversation | Shared by Scrum Team/product standard |
| Example: quote cannot be accepted after expiry | Example: tests pass, logs/metrics/audit ready, docs updated |

PO collaboration failure happens when acceptance is treated as Done.

Example:

- PO accepts UI behavior.
- Engineering did not add audit event.
- Support cannot reconstruct approval later.
- Increment is product-accepted but production-weak.

Senior engineer should help the PO see which DoD requirements protect product value.

## 13. Product Owner and spikes

Spikes should help the PO make better decisions.

A spike without a decision is often weak.

### 13.1 Good spike output for PO

A good spike should produce:

- decision options,
- recommended path,
- trade-offs,
- risk reduction,
- remaining unknowns,
- candidate PBIs,
- estimate confidence improvement.

Example spike:

> Investigate whether order cancellation can be safely added to the existing fulfillment adapter.

Output:

- option A: implement cancellation only before task submission,
- option B: implement downstream cancellation callback handling,
- option C: support manual cancellation only for phase 1,
- risk: downstream does not guarantee cancellation after accepted state,
- recommendation: ship phase 1 with pre-fulfillment cancellation and explicit rejection afterward.

This gives the PO a decision structure.

## 14. Product Owner and customer-specific behavior

Enterprise products often accumulate customer-specific requirements.

Senior engineers should help PO distinguish:

- product capability,
- customer configuration,
- customer extension,
- one-off workaround,
- custom integration,
- temporary exception,
- product anti-pattern.

### 14.1 Classification questions

Ask:

- Is this behavior valuable to many customers?
- Is this customer-specific due to contract obligation?
- Can it be modelled as configuration?
- Does it change core lifecycle semantics?
- Does it affect TMF/API compatibility?
- Does it create test matrix expansion?
- Does it require operational support?
- Who owns cleanup if temporary?

### 14.2 Risk of unclassified customization

Unclassified customization causes:

- hidden branches,
- brittle tests,
- unclear support behavior,
- poor upgrade path,
- customer-specific incidents,
- product roadmap distortion.

Senior engineers should not automatically reject customization.

They should make its lifecycle and support cost visible.

## 15. Product Owner and incidents

Production incidents are product feedback.

The PO should know when incidents reveal product/backlog issues.

### 15.1 Incident-to-backlog translation

| Incident finding | Backlog implication |
|---|---|
| Customers discover stuck orders first | Add alert/dashboard/runbook work. |
| Duplicate quote acceptance creates duplicate order | Prioritize idempotency and uniqueness guard. |
| Support cannot explain price | Improve pricing snapshot/audit evidence. |
| Migration failed for customer data shape | Add migration fixture and upgrade test. |
| Fulfillment callback lost | Add reconciliation/retry visibility. |

### 15.2 Post-incident PO conversation

Ask:

- Was customer value impacted?
- Was support cost high?
- Is prevention work product-critical?
- Does this change roadmap order?
- Do we need release note/customer communication?
- Should Definition of Done change?

A senior engineer helps make incidents visible without turning every bug into a panic.

## 16. Product Owner collaboration anti-patterns

### 16.1 Engineers demand perfect requirements

Bad:

> We cannot start until every edge case is specified.

Better:

> These three unknowns affect architecture and estimate. Let's run a spike or split the first slice to avoid committing to the full path prematurely.

### 16.2 Product Owner writes implementation tickets alone

Bad:

> Add column X, call service Y, publish event Z.

Better:

> Describe desired business outcome; engineering proposes implementation and technical slices.

### 16.3 Engineers hide technical debt

Bad:

> We will just clean it later.

Better:

> This implementation introduces a compatibility path. We need a cleanup PBI after migration completes.

### 16.4 PO accepts scope while engineering absorbs hidden quality loss

Bad:

> We can fit it if we skip tests/runbook.

Better:

> We can fit the core behavior, but not the full production readiness. Options: reduce scope, defer release, or explicitly accept risk.

### 16.5 Engineers overrule product value using technical preference

Bad:

> We should not do this because I dislike the design.

Better:

> This path has these risks and alternatives. If the customer deadline dominates, here is the smallest safe version.

## 17. Internal verification checklist

Verify these inside the team:

### 17.1 Product ownership structure

- Who is the Product Owner for the team?
- Is there also a Product Manager?
- Is there a Business Analyst role?
- Who owns roadmap ordering?
- Who owns acceptance of completed work?
- Who owns customer communication?
- Who owns customer-specific requirement validation?

### 17.2 Backlog practice

- Where is Product Backlog maintained?
- How are epics/features/stories structured?
- How are dependencies represented?
- How are technical debt items represented?
- How are production incidents converted into backlog items?
- How are spikes created and accepted?
- Are architecture PBIs allowed?

### 17.3 Refinement practice

- Who attends refinement?
- Are QA/security/platform/support included when needed?
- Are acceptance criteria reviewed before Sprint Planning?
- Are API/event/migration/security risks discussed?
- Is estimate confidence captured?
- Are open questions tracked?

### 17.4 Decision authority

- Who decides product scope?
- Who decides technical approach?
- Who decides release readiness?
- Who approves risk exceptions?
- Who handles conflict between product deadline and quality bar?
- Who decides customer-specific deviations?

## 18. Practical exercises

### Exercise 1 — Convert a vague PBI

Take a vague PBI from the backlog.

Rewrite it with:

- product outcome,
- actor,
- domain object,
- lifecycle state,
- acceptance criteria,
- technical risks,
- open questions,
- slicing options.

### Exercise 2 — Technical risk translation

Pick one technical debt item.

Translate it into:

- product impact,
- customer risk,
- support cost,
- roadmap dependency,
- suggested backlog priority.

### Exercise 3 — PO collaboration conversation

Prepare a 15-minute conversation with PO:

- one thing you think is unclear,
- one risk you think is hidden,
- one slicing option,
- one question about value/order.

Keep the tone collaborative.

### Exercise 4 — Incident-to-backlog mapping

Pick a recent production issue.

Map:

- symptom,
- root cause,
- customer impact,
- missing test/monitoring/runbook,
- backlog item for prevention.

## 19. What good looks like

After this part, you should be able to:

- collaborate with PO without role confusion,
- expose hidden engineering risk as product-relevant information,
- ask better refinement questions,
- help split large customer commitments,
- translate technical debt into backlog value,
- keep acceptance criteria and Definition of Done distinct,
- use incidents as product feedback,
- support PO decisions with evidence rather than opinion.

## 20. Final mental model

The Product Owner owns product value.

The senior engineer owns making technical reality visible enough that product value can be pursued safely.

In enterprise Quote & Order systems, that visibility is often the difference between a feature that merely ships and a product increment that can survive production, customer integration, audit, support, and future change.
