# Part 002 — Scrum Team Accountabilities for Senior Engineers

## 1. Source notes and scope boundary

This part uses public Scrum references and adapts them for senior engineers in enterprise product teams.

Primary public references to verify independently:

- The official Scrum Guide, which defines one Scrum Team with three accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers.
- Scrum.org explanations of accountability versus job title.
- Scrum.org materials on Product Owner accountability for maximizing product value and Scrum Master accountability for establishing Scrum.
- Engineering practice references on code review, code health, and team collaboration.

This part does **not** assume the internal CSG team has a pure Scrum role structure.

Many enterprise organizations have additional roles:

- engineering manager,
- team lead,
- tech lead,
- architect,
- QA engineer,
- business analyst,
- delivery manager,
- release manager,
- product manager,
- product owner proxy,
- support engineer,
- SRE/platform engineer,
- security engineer.

Scrum accountabilities and job titles may overlap, but they are not the same thing.

Use the `Internal verification checklist` sections to understand your actual team boundaries.

---

## 2. Core thesis

Scrum Team accountability is not about who has the most authority.

It is about making sure the product, the process, and the technical work are owned clearly enough that the team can deliver a useful, high-quality Increment.

For senior engineers, the key question is:

> How do I create technical leverage inside the Scrum Team without confusing my accountability with Product Owner, Scrum Master, manager, or architect?

A senior engineer must be able to:

- support product decisions without hijacking them,
- improve Scrum process without becoming the Scrum Master,
- provide technical direction without becoming a bottleneck,
- protect engineering quality without treating every preference as a blocker,
- mentor others without micromanaging,
- expose risk without spreading fear,
- negotiate trade-offs without owning the business priority alone.

This part teaches that boundary.

## 3. Accountabilities are not job titles

Scrum uses accountabilities, not job-title bureaucracy.

The three Scrum accountabilities are:

1. Product Owner,
2. Scrum Master,
3. Developers.

A person can have a company title that does not match the Scrum accountability name.

Examples:

| Company title | Possible Scrum accountability |
|---|---|
| Product Manager | Product Owner, or stakeholder to Product Owner |
| Business Analyst | Supports Product Owner and Developers |
| Senior Java Engineer | Developer, often with technical leadership leverage |
| QA Engineer | Developer, if part of Scrum Team creating Increment |
| Tech Lead | Developer with technical leadership responsibility |
| Engineering Manager | Usually outside Scrum accountabilities unless also on Scrum Team |
| Scrum Master | Scrum Master accountability |
| Delivery Manager | May support flow, but not necessarily Scrum Master |
| Architect | Could be Developer, advisor, or external governance role |

The important thing is not the title.

The important thing is clarity:

- Who maximizes product value?
- Who establishes and improves Scrum?
- Who creates the Increment?
- Who decides priority?
- Who decides technical approach?
- Who approves release?
- Who owns production support?
- Who owns architecture standards?

When these are unclear, Scrum becomes theatre.

## 4. Product Owner accountability

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work.

In practice, this often includes:

- Product Goal clarity,
- Product Backlog ordering,
- backlog item clarity,
- stakeholder alignment,
- prioritization,
- value trade-offs,
- acceptance of outcomes,
- product feedback loop.

For Quote & Order systems, Product Owner work may involve:

- customer commitments,
- CPQ capability roadmap,
- order management roadmap,
- pricing and catalog priorities,
- support escalations,
- regulatory/customer-specific expectations,
- stakeholder communication,
- release scope decisions.

## 4.1 What senior engineers should do to support the Product Owner

Senior engineers should help the PO understand:

- technical risk,
- domain complexity,
- implementation options,
- dependency chains,
- migration cost,
- operational cost,
- testing cost,
- sequencing constraints,
- delivery trade-offs.

Good senior engineer support:

> This requirement has two slices. Slice A supports the new discount rule only for new quotes behind a feature flag and needs no migration. Slice B applies it to existing draft quotes and requires backfill plus approval invalidation rules. I recommend we prioritize Slice A first to validate behavior safely.

Weak support:

> This is hard. We need more time.

Strong support turns technical complexity into decision-ready options.

## 4.2 What senior engineers should not do

Do not silently become the Product Owner.

Anti-patterns:

- deciding product priority alone,
- accepting requirements without PO/stakeholder validation,
- changing scope without product awareness,
- hiding trade-offs inside implementation,
- implementing technical preference as product behavior,
- saying "the business wants this" without evidence.

A senior engineer can recommend strongly, but the product value decision belongs to Product Ownership.

## 4.3 Useful questions to ask the PO

For any significant Quote & Order item:

- What customer or business problem does this solve?
- Which customer segment or tenant is affected?
- Is this required for a contractual commitment?
- Is this cloud-only, on-prem, or both?
- Is this a general capability or customer-specific behavior?
- What happens if we deliver only the first slice?
- What is the smallest valuable increment?
- What is unacceptable behavior?
- Which stakeholder should inspect the increment?
- What should be shown in Sprint Review?

## 5. Scrum Master accountability

The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide.

In practice, this may involve:

- coaching Scrum theory and practice,
- helping the team improve self-management,
- facilitating events when needed,
- removing impediments,
- helping Product Owner with backlog management,
- helping organization understand Scrum,
- improving transparency and adaptation.

## 5.1 What senior engineers should do to support the Scrum Master

Senior engineers can help the Scrum Master by making engineering process problems visible.

Examples:

- PR review is a bottleneck.
- Refinement misses migration risk.
- Sprint Planning ignores support load.
- Retrospective actions are not followed up.
- Too much WIP creates context switching.
- Stories are too large to inspect.
- Testing is delayed until after development.
- Production incidents are not converted into backlog improvements.

Good senior engineer support:

> In the last three sprints, two stories carried over because API contract review happened after implementation. Can we add an API/event impact question to refinement for integration stories?

This is not taking over Scrum Master accountability.

It is providing evidence to improve Scrum.

## 5.2 What senior engineers should not do

Do not weaponize Scrum language.

Anti-patterns:

- using Scrum rules to shut down conversation,
- treating the Scrum Master as meeting secretary,
- blaming Scrum Master for all delivery problems,
- using velocity as pressure,
- forcing every improvement through ceremony,
- avoiding technical leadership by saying "Scrum says self-managing".

Scrum Master supports the process.

Developers, including senior engineers, still own technical execution.

## 6. Developers accountability

In Scrum, Developers are accountable for creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint.

Developers may include engineers, QA, analysts, designers, database specialists, or other skills needed to create the Increment.

For a Senior Java Engineer, Developer accountability includes:

- implementation,
- tests,
- design collaboration,
- integration,
- code review,
- technical quality,
- production readiness,
- maintainability,
- security-aware implementation,
- observability,
- helping the team meet Definition of Done.

## 6.1 Senior engineer nuance

A senior engineer is a Developer in Scrum, but with extra leverage.

The senior engineer is not more accountable than the team for the Increment, but can increase the team's ability to deliver it.

Senior leverage includes:

- seeing hidden technical risk,
- identifying better slices,
- mentoring others,
- improving review quality,
- shaping design,
- reducing dependency confusion,
- improving production readiness,
- making architecture trade-offs clear.

## 6.2 Developers own the plan for the Sprint

The Product Owner orders the Product Backlog.

Developers decide how to turn selected work into a done Increment.

This distinction matters.

Example:

PO says:

> We need quote expiration behavior for enterprise customers.

Developers decide:

- whether a spike is needed,
- which code paths change,
- how to slice the work,
- what tests are required,
- whether migration is required,
- how to sequence feature flag rollout,
- how to update observability,
- how to meet Definition of Done.

A senior engineer should help Developers create a realistic implementation plan.

## 7. Where tech lead fits

Scrum does not define a tech lead accountability.

But many enterprise teams have tech leads.

A tech lead may be responsible for:

- technical direction,
- architecture consistency,
- design review,
- code quality,
- mentoring,
- cross-team technical alignment,
- production technical escalation.

The risk is confusion.

## 7.1 Healthy tech lead behavior

A healthy tech lead:

- clarifies technical options,
- builds consensus,
- helps unblock,
- delegates effectively,
- raises quality standards,
- writes/encourages ADRs,
- ensures risky work is reviewed,
- avoids becoming the only decision maker.

## 7.2 Unhealthy tech lead behavior

An unhealthy tech lead:

- becomes the mandatory reviewer for everything,
- overrides Product Owner priority without conversation,
- bypasses Scrum Master/process improvement,
- makes architecture decisions privately,
- creates dependency bottleneck,
- gives solutions without context,
- prevents team ownership.

## 7.3 Senior engineer relationship with tech lead

As a senior engineer, your goal is not to compete with the tech lead.

Your goal is to create aligned leverage.

Ask:

- Which area needs my ownership?
- Which decisions should I escalate?
- Which PRs need your review?
- Which design standards should I follow?
- Where should I mentor others?
- Which technical debt is highest priority?

## 8. Where engineering manager fits

Scrum does not define engineering manager as a Scrum accountability.

The engineering manager often owns:

- people management,
- hiring,
- performance feedback,
- career growth,
- team health,
- organizational alignment,
- sometimes delivery accountability,
- sometimes operational escalation.

A senior engineer should communicate with the manager about:

- team bottlenecks,
- mentoring needs,
- ownership gaps,
- technical risk,
- capacity constraints,
- recurring incidents,
- cross-team dependency issues.

Do not confuse manager escalation with avoiding team self-management.

Use manager help for systemic or organizational blockers.

---

## 9. Where QA/testing fits

In Scrum, quality is not an external phase.

If QA engineers are part of the Scrum Team, they are Developers contributing to the Increment.

Senior engineers should treat QA as engineering partners, especially in enterprise systems.

QA can help with:

- acceptance criteria clarity,
- edge cases,
- regression strategy,
- E2E journey design,
- test data strategy,
- exploratory testing,
- release confidence,
- defect triage.

Bad pattern:

> Dev finishes coding, throws to QA at the end.

Good pattern:

> Engineer, QA, and PO refine acceptance criteria together before Sprint Planning. Test strategy is part of the implementation plan.

For Quote & Order, QA involvement is crucial for:

- lifecycle state transitions,
- pricing combinations,
- catalog variants,
- customer-specific behavior,
- order fallout,
- integration failure paths,
- regression suites.

## 10. Where architecture/security/platform roles fit

Enterprise teams often depend on specialized roles.

### Architecture

Architecture may review:

- cross-service design,
- API/event standards,
- data ownership,
- integration patterns,
- non-functional requirements,
- migration strategy.

Senior engineer responsibility:

- involve architecture early for high-risk changes,
- write design docs/ADRs,
- avoid surprise late review.

### Security

Security may review:

- authentication,
- authorization,
- tenant isolation,
- secrets,
- PII handling,
- audit/compliance,
- threat model.

Senior engineer responsibility:

- know when security review is required,
- include negative tests,
- avoid treating security as afterthought.

### Platform/SRE

Platform/SRE may own:

- Kubernetes standards,
- CI/CD pipelines,
- observability platform,
- infrastructure,
- incident process,
- deployment guardrails.

Senior engineer responsibility:

- understand platform constraints,
- involve platform for runtime changes,
- make production readiness visible.

## 11. Accountability boundaries in common scenarios

## 11.1 Vague backlog item

Scenario:

> Add support for enterprise order cancellation.

Product Owner accountability:

- clarify product value,
- prioritize cancellation scenarios,
- align stakeholders,
- define acceptable business outcomes.

Developers accountability:

- identify technical impact,
- propose slices,
- define implementation plan,
- estimate uncertainty,
- identify tests/migrations/events.

Scrum Master accountability:

- help team improve refinement,
- ensure transparency,
- facilitate collaboration if needed.

Senior engineer leverage:

- ask state-machine questions,
- surface fulfillment/billing/audit risks,
- propose spike or vertical slice,
- avoid premature implementation.

## 11.2 Unsafe migration discovered mid-sprint

Scenario:

A migration planned for a story would lock a hot PostgreSQL table.

Product Owner accountability:

- decide priority trade-off with new information,
- adjust scope if necessary.

Developers accountability:

- explain risk,
- propose safer migration sequence,
- update plan,
- ensure tests/dry-run.

Scrum Master accountability:

- support adaptation,
- help remove impediments,
- protect transparency.

Senior engineer leverage:

- recommend expand-contract,
- create technical spike/backlog item,
- explain release risk,
- prevent unsafe merge.

## 11.3 Production incident during Sprint

Scenario:

Kafka consumer bug causes orders to remain stuck in fallout.

Product Owner accountability:

- understand product/customer impact,
- help reorder backlog if incident work affects Sprint Goal.

Developers accountability:

- investigate,
- mitigate,
- repair,
- add prevention work.

Scrum Master accountability:

- facilitate adaptation,
- make interruption visible,
- help team maintain focus after triage.

Senior engineer leverage:

- lead diagnosis if appropriate,
- protect team from unstructured chaos,
- ensure post-incident actions become backlog items,
- update runbook/tests/observability.

## 11.4 Architecture disagreement

Scenario:

Team disagrees whether to implement new integration through synchronous REST call or event-driven workflow.

Product Owner accountability:

- clarify business latency/consistency needs,
- decide product trade-offs with team input.

Developers accountability:

- analyze technical options,
- model failure modes,
- recommend approach,
- document decision.

Scrum Master accountability:

- support effective decision process if conversation stalls.

Senior engineer leverage:

- shift debate from taste to criteria:
  - consistency requirement,
  - failure mode,
  - retry behavior,
  - customer visibility,
  - operational cost,
  - testability,
  - migration path.

## 12. RACI-style map for enterprise Scrum work

RACI is not Scrum-native, but it can clarify enterprise collaboration.

Use carefully.

Example map:

| Activity | Product Owner | Scrum Master | Developers | Senior Engineer | Architect/Security/Platform |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product priority | Accountable | Supports process | Consulted | Consulted | Consulted |
| Backlog clarity | Accountable | Facilitates | Responsible | Helps de-risk | Consulted |
| Technical design | Consulted | Supports process | Responsible | Often leads | Consulted/approves where required |
| Implementation | Consulted | Removes impediments | Accountable as team | Contributes/leads | Consulted |
| Testing strategy | Consulted | Supports improvement | Responsible | Shapes risk-based approach | Consulted |
| Production readiness | Consulted | Helps transparency | Responsible | Often drives evidence | Platform/SRE consulted |
| Sprint Goal | Collaborates | Facilitates | Collaborates | Helps feasibility | Consulted where relevant |
| Release decision | Depends on org | Depends on org | Provides evidence | Provides risk assessment | Depends on org |

The map is not universal.

Verify internal rules.

## 13. Senior engineer collaboration patterns

## 13.1 With Product Owner

Useful behaviors:

- translate technical risk into product impact,
- offer options with trade-offs,
- identify smallest valuable increment,
- clarify hidden edge cases,
- avoid product decision takeover.

Example:

> Option A supports cancellation only before fulfillment starts. It is smaller and safer. Option B supports cancellation during fulfillment but requires downstream compensation and fallout handling. I recommend Option A first unless the customer commitment requires mid-fulfillment cancellation now.

## 13.2 With Scrum Master

Useful behaviors:

- provide evidence of process bottlenecks,
- help improve refinement and DoD,
- make blockers visible,
- support retro actions,
- avoid treating Scrum Master as project secretary.

Example:

> Our blocked time is mostly review waiting. Can we try assigning domain reviewers during Sprint Planning for high-risk stories?

## 13.3 With Developers

Useful behaviors:

- pair on complex areas,
- mentor through reasoning,
- review with context,
- share ownership,
- avoid becoming the only person who can merge.

Example:

> Let's walk through the order transition invariant before coding. After that, you can implement the first path and I will review for lifecycle/audit consistency.

## 13.4 With QA

Useful behaviors:

- involve QA during refinement,
- create testable acceptance criteria,
- share domain edge cases,
- build stable fixtures,
- treat defects as learning.

Example:

> This story needs tests for accepted quote, expired quote, and approved-but-modified quote. Let's define those before implementation.

## 13.5 With platform/SRE

Useful behaviors:

- discuss runtime impact early,
- verify deployment/migration concerns,
- include observability/runbook requirements,
- avoid surprise production changes.

Example:

> This consumer change may increase Kafka lag during replay. Can we review resource limits and dashboard before enabling it?

## 14. Anti-patterns around accountabilities

## 14.1 Product Owner as ticket writer only

Symptoms:

- PO writes acceptance criteria but does not own value trade-offs,
- engineers decide all priority implicitly,
- Sprint Review has little stakeholder feedback.

Senior response:

- ask outcome-oriented questions,
- show trade-offs,
- invite product decision rather than bypassing it.

## 14.2 Scrum Master as meeting scheduler only

Symptoms:

- Scrum Master only books meetings,
- impediments repeat,
- retrospectives do not improve anything,
- transparency is weak.

Senior response:

- bring evidence of process problems,
- collaborate on improvement experiments,
- support team self-management.

## 14.3 Senior engineer as shadow Product Owner

Symptoms:

- senior engineer chooses scope without PO,
- technical convenience becomes product behavior,
- stakeholders surprised in Sprint Review.

Senior response:

- separate recommendation from decision,
- document assumptions,
- involve PO early.

## 14.4 Tech lead as bottleneck

Symptoms:

- all PRs wait for one person,
- decisions are private,
- team avoids ownership,
- knowledge concentrates dangerously.

Senior response:

- distribute review ownership,
- document decisions,
- mentor area owners,
- make escalation criteria clear.

## 14.5 QA as end-of-sprint gate

Symptoms:

- QA receives work too late,
- bugs spill into next sprint,
- stories are "dev done" but not done,
- test data issues discovered late.

Senior response:

- involve QA in refinement,
- define test strategy early,
- support automation and fixture quality.

## 14.6 Architect as late-stage approver

Symptoms:

- design review happens after implementation,
- rework is expensive,
- teams avoid architecture review because it feels blocking.

Senior response:

- create lightweight design notes early,
- involve architecture for high-risk changes before Sprint commitment,
- use ADRs for durable decisions.

## 15. Senior engineer decision boundaries

A senior engineer should know when to decide, when to recommend, and when to escalate.

## 15.1 Decide locally

Appropriate when:

- change is within owned code area,
- no public contract impact,
- no security/data/migration risk,
- consistent with existing standards,
- reversible.

Examples:

- refactor internal method,
- improve test fixture,
- add missing validation test,
- improve logging field.

## 15.2 Recommend to team/PO

Appropriate when:

- product behavior trade-off exists,
- scope sequencing matters,
- customer-visible behavior changes,
- roadmap priority may be affected.

Examples:

- choose partial cancellation support first,
- defer existing quote migration,
- split feature by tenant/customer segment.

## 15.3 Escalate to architecture/security/platform

Appropriate when:

- public API/event contract changes,
- database migration is high risk,
- tenant isolation/security boundary changes,
- cross-team dependency exists,
- operational platform behavior changes,
- customer/on-prem release path changes.

## 15.4 Escalate to manager/leadership

Appropriate when:

- capacity is structurally insufficient,
- team ownership is unclear,
- repeated process issue cannot be solved locally,
- high-risk decision needs organizational agreement,
- support load is consuming sprint capacity invisibly.

## 16. Internal verification checklist

### 16.1 Accountabilities and titles

- Who is the official Product Owner?
- Is there also a Product Manager?
- Is there a Business Analyst?
- Who is the Scrum Master?
- Is there a delivery manager?
- Is there a tech lead?
- Is there an engineering manager?
- Are QA engineers part of the Scrum Team?
- Are architects embedded or external?
- Are platform/SRE engineers embedded or shared?

### 16.2 Decision rights

- Who orders the Product Backlog?
- Who accepts work as meeting product expectations?
- Who decides technical design?
- Who approves architecture changes?
- Who approves API/event contract changes?
- Who approves DB migrations?
- Who approves security-sensitive changes?
- Who approves release readiness?
- Who can stop a release?

### 16.3 Collaboration patterns

- When does refinement happen?
- Who attends refinement?
- Are QA/support/platform/security involved when needed?
- How are dependencies tracked?
- How are blockers escalated?
- How are mid-sprint changes handled?
- How are incidents represented in sprint work?

### 16.4 Engineering expectations

- What does the team expect from a Senior Java Engineer?
- What does the team expect from a tech lead?
- What review depth is expected?
- Is mentoring expected?
- Is production/on-call ownership expected?
- How are design docs/ADRs used?
- How are technical debt items prioritized?

### 16.5 Scrum health

- Is Sprint Goal meaningful?
- Is Product Backlog ordered and clear enough?
- Is Definition of Done real?
- Are Retrospective actions followed up?
- Are Sprint Reviews stakeholder-relevant?
- Are Daily Scrums useful for Developers?
- Are accountabilities clear or blurred?

- Am I becoming a review bottleneck?
- Am I helping QA early enough?
- Am I escalating architecture/security/platform risk early enough?

## 18. What good looks like after this part

After this part, you should be able to:

- distinguish Scrum accountabilities from company job titles,
- explain how Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers collaborate,
- support Product Owner with technical trade-off clarity without taking over product priority,
- support Scrum Master with process evidence without treating them as meeting secretary,
- act as a senior Developer who improves the team's ability to create done increments,
- collaborate effectively with QA, architecture, security, platform, and management,
- know when to decide, recommend, or escalate,
- reduce accountability confusion inside Scrum delivery.

## 19. Key takeaways

Scrum Team accountabilities are designed to make ownership clear.

Enterprise organizations add job titles and governance layers, which can make ownership blurry.

A senior engineer creates leverage by clarifying boundaries:

- Product Owner owns value and ordering.
- Scrum Master owns Scrum effectiveness.
- Developers own creating the usable Increment.
- Senior engineers contribute technical judgment, risk discovery, mentoring, design clarity, and production-quality execution.

The goal is not to control everything.

The goal is to make the team more capable of delivering valuable, safe, inspectable increments without hidden risk.
