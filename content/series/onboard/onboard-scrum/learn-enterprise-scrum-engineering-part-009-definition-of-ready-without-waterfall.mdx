# Part 009 — Definition of Ready Without Waterfall

## 1. Source notes and scope boundary

This part uses public Scrum guidance and common enterprise engineering practice.

Public references to verify independently:

- The Scrum Guide defines Product Backlog refinement as breaking down and further defining Product Backlog Items into smaller, more precise items.
- The Scrum Guide says Product Backlog Items that can be Done by the Scrum Team within one Sprint are deemed ready for selection in Sprint Planning.
- Scrum.org has public articles explaining that Definition of Ready can be useful as a set of criteria for selecting Product Backlog Items, but it is not a formal mandatory Scrum artifact like the Definition of Done.
- Scrum.org articles often warn that Definition of Ready can become a harmful phase-gate if used to block learning, collaboration, or empiricism.

This part does **not** assume CSG uses an official Definition of Ready checklist, Jira workflow gate, Scrum Master policy, or Product Owner readiness rule.

Use the internal verification checklist to discover your team's actual expectations.

---

## 2. Core thesis

Definition of Ready is useful when it creates enough clarity to start meaningful work.

Definition of Ready is harmful when it becomes a bureaucratic wall that prevents the Scrum Team from learning during the Sprint.

For senior engineers in enterprise Quote & Order work, the goal is not:

> Every requirement must be perfectly specified before Sprint Planning.

The goal is:

> The team understands enough about value, risk, scope, dependency, acceptance, and production impact to make a responsible Sprint commitment.

That distinction matters.

A complex CPQ/order story often contains uncertainty:

- business rule uncertainty,
- catalog versioning uncertainty,
- pricing/discount edge cases,
- approval authority rules,
- order lifecycle ambiguity,
- Kafka event compatibility,
- PostgreSQL migration risk,
- tenant isolation impact,
- customer-specific behavior,
- deployment and support constraints.

A healthy Definition of Ready does not eliminate all uncertainty.

It makes uncertainty visible, bounded, and manageable.

## 3. Definition of Ready vs Definition of Done

Definition of Ready and Definition of Done are not symmetric.

| Concept | Purpose | Formal Scrum artifact? | Direction |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Definition of Done | Defines quality required for the Increment | Yes | Completion quality |
| Definition of Ready | Team-created clarity threshold for selecting work | No formal Scrum artifact | Starting clarity |

Definition of Done is a commitment associated with the Increment.

Definition of Ready is usually a working agreement.

This difference is important.

A weak team may misuse DoR as a reason to reject collaboration:

> The story is not ready. Product must fix it alone.

A strong team uses DoR as a collaboration trigger:

> This story has unresolved catalog-versioning and migration risk. Let's refine those before we pull it into the Sprint, or intentionally create a spike if the uncertainty is too high.

## 4. The real purpose of DoR

A good DoR helps answer six questions.

### 4.1 Value clarity

Do we understand what product/customer/business outcome this item supports?

For Quote & Order:

- Is this about faster quote creation?
- Better pricing accuracy?
- Fewer fallout orders?
- Safer approval governance?
- Better TMF API compatibility?
- Reduced support burden?
- Customer-specific rollout?

If the value is vague, scope decisions become random.

### 4.2 Scope clarity

Do we know what is in and out?

Example:

> "Improve quote approval" is not ready.

Better:

> "For enterprise quotes where requested discount exceeds the user's approval threshold, prevent quote acceptance until an authorized approver approves the exact quote version."

Scope clarity prevents sprint work from expanding mid-flight.

### 4.3 Acceptance clarity

Do we know how the team, PO, QA, and stakeholders will recognize success?

Acceptance criteria should include:

- happy path,
- negative path,
- edge case,
- audit expectation,
- API behavior,
- event behavior if relevant,
- migration or compatibility expectation,
- observability expectation when needed.

### 4.4 Dependency clarity

Do we know what this item depends on?

Dependencies may include:

- catalog data,
- pricing service,
- customer/account context,
- approval policy,
- fulfillment adapter,
- downstream Kafka consumer,
- database migration,
- feature flag,
- platform deployment,
- security review,
- customer-specific config.

DoR does not require all dependencies to be resolved.

It requires the team to know which dependencies are unresolved and whether the Sprint can absorb them.

### 4.5 Risk clarity

Do we know the main risk category?

Risk categories:

- business rule risk,
- lifecycle state risk,
- data migration risk,
- API compatibility risk,
- Kafka event compatibility risk,
- performance risk,
- security/tenant risk,
- operational readiness risk,
- customer rollout risk.

If risk is unknown, refinement is incomplete.

### 4.6 Sizing clarity

Can the item plausibly be completed within one Sprint according to the Definition of Done?

If not, the item may need:

- story slicing,
- spike,
- dependency removal,
- feature flag strategy,
- partial vertical slice,
- separate migration item,
- separate contract/test item.

## 5. DoR is a clarity threshold, not a guarantee

Even a ready story may discover new information during the Sprint.

That is normal.

The Scrum framework is empirical. It expects learning through inspection and adaptation.

A story is ready enough when:

- the team can start without reckless guessing,
- uncertainty is named,
- major dependencies are visible,
- acceptance criteria are testable,
- the item is small enough,
- the expected Increment can meet the Definition of Done,
- the team knows how to escalate new discoveries.

A story is not ready when:

- nobody can explain the user/customer/business value,
- the scope is a slogan,
- acceptance criteria are missing or untestable,
- external dependency is unknown,
- required API/event/migration/security impact is hidden,
- the work is too large for one Sprint,
- the team knows it will need a design decision but no path exists.

## 6. Lightweight DoR for enterprise backend work

Use a small checklist that detects real risk.

### 6.1 Minimum DoR

A Product Backlog Item is ready enough for Sprint Planning when:

1. The value/outcome is understood.
2. The primary user/system actor is known.
3. The scope is small enough for one Sprint.
4. Acceptance criteria are testable.
5. Major dependencies are identified.
6. Main technical risk is named.
7. Expected tests are understood.
8. Any required design/spike/migration decision is explicit.
9. The team believes the item can meet the Definition of Done.

This is intentionally not a 40-point checklist.

The more complex the item, the more detailed the readiness conversation should be.

### 6.2 Extended DoR for Quote & Order work

Use the extended checklist when the item touches CPQ/order domain behavior.

| Area | Readiness question |
|---|---|
| Domain | Which quote/order/catalog/pricing/approval invariant is affected? |
| Actor | Which user/service/system initiates this behavior? |
| State | Which lifecycle state allows or rejects this behavior? |
| API | Does REST/OpenAPI/TMF contract change? |
| Event | Does Kafka topic/schema/partition key/event semantic change? |
| Data | Does PostgreSQL schema/query/migration/backfill change? |
| Security | Does auth, RBAC, approval authority, or tenant isolation change? |
| Audit | Does the action require audit evidence? |
| Observability | What signal proves it works or fails in production? |
| Rollout | Is feature flag/config/customer rollout needed? |
| Support | Does support need new runbook or diagnostic path? |

A story does not need every area.

But if an area applies, readiness should not ignore it.

## 7. Examples of weak vs strong readiness

### 7.1 Weak story

```md
As a sales user, I want quote approval to be improved so that approval works better.
```

Why it is not ready:

- value vague,
- actor vague,
- approval rule unknown,
- affected state unknown,
- acceptance criteria missing,
- no audit expectation,
- no security/authority model,
- no test strategy.

### 7.2 Stronger story

```md
As a sales user creating an enterprise quote,
I want the system to require approval when the discount exceeds my allowed threshold,
so that accepted quotes preserve commercial governance.
```

Acceptance criteria:

- Given a quote discount below threshold, approval is not required.
- Given a quote discount above threshold, quote cannot be accepted until approved.
- Given a user without sufficient authority, approval attempt is denied.
- Given an authorized approver, approval records actor, reason, threshold, and quote version.
- Given quote price or discount changes after approval, previous approval becomes invalid.
- Audit event is written for approval and invalidation.
- API response returns stable error code when acceptance is blocked.

Readiness improved because it names:

- business rule,
- actor,
- state transition,
- authority,
- audit,
- negative path,
- contract behavior.

## 8. DoR anti-patterns

### 8.1 Waterfall DoR

Symptom:

> Every detail must be fully specified before Developers engage.

Why harmful:

- delays collaboration,
- hides uncertainty until late,
- separates business and engineering thinking,
- creates large requirement documents,
- prevents empirical learning.

Better:

- include Developers early in refinement,
- identify uncertainty,
- split discovery from delivery when needed,
- create small slices.

### 8.2 Checkbox DoR

Symptom:

> All Jira fields are filled, but nobody understands the item.

Why harmful:

- creates false readiness,
- encourages bureaucracy,
- weakens conversation.

Better:

- use DoR as conversation prompts,
- ask people to explain behavior and risk in plain language.

### 8.3 PO-only readiness

Symptom:

> Product Owner is expected to make items ready alone.

Why harmful:

- technical risk appears too late,
- estimates become guesswork,
- architecture dependencies are missed.

Better:

- refinement is Scrum Team work,
- senior engineers contribute risk and slicing insight,
- QA/support/platform input is pulled when needed.

### 8.4 Design hidden inside delivery story

Symptom:

> Story includes implementation, discovery, architecture decision, migration, contract design, and testing all in one Sprint item.

Why harmful:

- estimate unreliable,
- Sprint Goal risk high,
- partial discovery causes scope explosion.

Better:

- split into discovery spike and delivery slice,
- timebox uncertainty,
- require a decision artifact from spike.

### 8.5 Ready means no uncertainty

Symptom:

> Team refuses to start any item with unknowns.

Why harmful:

- complex work never starts,
- learning is not treated as work,
- backlog becomes stale.

Better:

- classify uncertainty,
- decide if it can be handled in Sprint,
- use spikes for high uncertainty,
- keep Sprint Goal realistic.

## 9. DoR by work type

Different work needs different readiness signals.

### 9.1 Feature story

Ready when:

- business outcome clear,
- actor clear,
- scope sliced,
- acceptance criteria testable,
- DoD achievable,
- dependencies known.

### 9.2 API story

Ready when:

- endpoint/resource behavior clear,
- request/response examples available,
- error behavior clear,
- backward compatibility assessed,
- auth/tenant scope clear,
- contract tests planned.

### 9.3 Kafka/event story

Ready when:

- producer and consumer known,
- topic/schema/partition key impact known,
- event semantic clear,
- replay/idempotency expectation clear,
- compatibility and DLQ/retry impact assessed.

### 9.4 Database migration story

Ready when:

- schema/data change understood,
- backward compatibility strategy known,
- migration/backfill approach named,
- lock/runtime risk assessed,
- rollback/roll-forward expectation known,
- test strategy defined.

### 9.5 Refactoring story

Ready when:

- behavior to preserve is characterized,
- risk is explicit,
- boundary/seam is known,
- test coverage strategy exists,
- rollback path or incremental path exists,
- expected improvement is measurable.

### 9.6 Production bug story

Ready when:

- symptom reproduced or evidence captured,
- impact understood,
- affected tenants/customers known if possible,
- suspected root cause named or investigation path defined,
- fix and prevention expectation clear,
- data repair need considered.

### 9.7 Spike story

Ready when:

- hypothesis/question clear,
- timebox clear,
- output clear,
- decision criteria clear,
- owner/reviewer clear,
- follow-up path clear.

Part 010 expands this in detail.

## 10. Senior engineer role in DoR

A senior engineer should improve readiness without becoming a blocker.

### 10.1 Ask domain-risk questions

Examples:

- Which quote/order state does this affect?
- Does this change accepted quote behavior?
- Can old quotes/orders still progress after release?
- Does this change approval evidence?
- Is catalog versioning involved?
- Does fulfillment or billing depend on this event?

### 10.2 Ask technical-risk questions

Examples:

- Is this an API contract change?
- Does this require migration/backfill?
- Is there a Kafka event schema change?
- Is idempotency required?
- Does this touch tenant isolation?
- Is the affected query hot?
- Does the test environment support this scenario?

### 10.3 Help split work

If the item is too large, suggest slices:

- discovery spike,
- API contract slice,
- internal domain slice,
- migration expand slice,
- backfill slice,
- read-path slice,
- write-path slice,
- feature-flagged behavior slice,
- observability/runbook slice.

### 10.4 Name assumptions

Do not say:

> This is unclear.

Say:

> This story assumes quote acceptance always happens after approval, but existing customers may have auto-approval paths. We should verify that before Sprint Planning or slice this story to one customer segment.

Specific assumptions create useful refinement.

## 11. Readiness scoring model

For complex items, use a lightweight scoring model.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value | unknown | partially clear | clear outcome |
| Scope | vague | bounded but broad | one-sprint slice |
| Acceptance | missing | partial | testable |
| Dependency | unknown | known but unresolved | known and planned |
| Risk | unknown | named | mitigated or intentionally accepted |
| Testability | unclear | possible | planned |
| Production impact | ignored | suspected | explicitly considered |

Interpretation:

- mostly 0: not ready; needs refinement;
- mostly 1: maybe ready if Sprint Goal tolerates uncertainty;
- mostly 2: ready enough;
- any critical 0 in security/data/migration/API may block selection.

Do not turn this into a performance metric.

Use it as a conversation tool.

## 12. Example: making a migration item ready

Weak item:

```md
Add approval_basis_hash to quote table.
```

Questions:

- Why is this needed?
- Is it nullable first?
- How are existing approved quotes backfilled?
- Does old app tolerate the column?
- Does new app tolerate null while backfill runs?
- Is it part of audit/approval evidence?
- Is it calculated from stable data?
- Does it need index?
- Does migration lock hot table?
- Does on-prem upgrade need special handling?

Ready item:

```md
As a platform/product engineer,
I want to add a nullable approval_basis_hash column and write it for new approvals,
so that future releases can verify that approval applies to the exact quote commercial basis.
```

Acceptance criteria:

- Migration adds nullable column without blocking quote writes beyond acceptable lock window.
- New approval writes hash for newly approved quotes.
- Existing approved quotes may remain null in this slice.
- Application read path tolerates null.
- No public API behavior changes.
- Integration test verifies new approval writes hash.
- Migration test verifies old fixture upgrades.
- Follow-up backfill story is created.

This is ready because it is sliced safely.

## 13. Example: making an event story ready

Weak item:

```md
Add quote accepted event.
```

Questions:

- Who consumes it?
- What business fact does it represent?
- What topic?
- What partition key?
- What schema version?
- Is it emitted after commit?
- Is duplicate acceptance possible?
- Does it include quote version and tenant?
- Does it include pricing snapshot reference?
- Is it replay-safe?

Ready item:

```md
As downstream order orchestration,
I want a QuoteAccepted event emitted after the quote acceptance transaction commits,
so that order creation can be triggered from a durable accepted quote fact.
```

Acceptance criteria:

- Event is emitted only after quote acceptance commit.
- Event includes tenant ID, quote ID, quote version, event ID, correlation ID, causation ID.
- Event partition key preserves per-quote ordering.
- Duplicate accept command does not create duplicate logical event.
- Event schema is backward compatible.
- Consumer contract test exists or consumer expectation is documented.
- Outbox relay failure is observable.

This is ready because it identifies producer/consumer, event semantic, and safety constraints.

## 14. DoR meeting behavior

A good refinement conversation sounds like this:

- "What is the smallest business outcome here?"
- "Which lifecycle states are included?"
- "What is explicitly out of scope?"
- "What would make this fail in production?"
- "Is this an API/event/data/security change?"
- "Can we complete this according to DoD inside one Sprint?"
- "Do we need a spike before committing?"
- "What would we demo in Sprint Review?"

A weak refinement conversation sounds like this:

- "How many points?"
- "Can we just take it?"
- "We'll figure it out during sprint."
- "QA will test later."
- "It's just backend."
- "No need to document."

Senior engineers should shift conversation from estimation-first to readiness-first.

## 15. Internal verification checklist

Validate these inside your actual CSG/team context:

### 15.1 Team working agreement

- Does the team have an explicit Definition of Ready?
- Is DoR written down?
- Is it used as guidance or hard gate?
- Who can say an item is not ready?
- How are unresolved dependencies handled?
- Are spikes allowed in the Sprint Backlog?
- Are design tasks visible in the Product Backlog?

### 15.2 Tooling and workflow

- Which Jira/Azure fields indicate readiness?
- Are acceptance criteria mandatory?
- Are dependencies tracked explicitly?
- Are architecture/security/release labels used?
- Is blocked/refinement-needed work visible?
- Are bugs and incidents refined differently?

### 15.3 Engineering quality

- Does readiness include test expectations?
- Does readiness include API/event compatibility when applicable?
- Does readiness include migration/backfill strategy?
- Does readiness include observability/runbook expectations?
- Does readiness include security/tenant/audit expectations?

### 15.4 Scrum culture

- Does DoR improve collaboration?
- Does DoR create handoff behavior?
- Does DoR delay learning unnecessarily?
- Are Developers engaged early enough?
- Does PO get enough technical support during refinement?

## 16. Practical exercises

### Exercise 1 — Audit five backlog items

Pick five upcoming Product Backlog Items.

Score each on:

- value clarity,
- scope clarity,
- acceptance clarity,
- dependency clarity,
- risk clarity,
- testability,
- production impact.

For each weak item, propose one refinement question.

### Exercise 2 — Rewrite one vague item

Take a vague story like:

> Improve order cancellation.

Rewrite it into:

- actor,
- business outcome,
- included states,
- excluded states,
- acceptance criteria,
- tests,
- production signal.

### Exercise 3 — Identify hidden waterfall behavior

Observe one refinement/planning cycle.

Look for:

- PO-only preparation,
- engineering late involvement,
- giant stories,
- no uncertainty handling,
- design hidden inside delivery,
- QA after-the-fact testing.

Suggest one small process improvement.

## 17. Summary

Definition of Ready is valuable when it helps the Scrum Team make responsible Sprint selections.

For enterprise Quote & Order work, readiness means enough clarity about:

- business value,
- scope,
- acceptance,
- dependency,
- risk,
- testability,
- production impact.

It does not mean every detail is known.

A senior engineer uses DoR to expose hidden risk, improve story slicing, and protect the Sprint Goal.

The best DoR is lightweight, collaborative, and empirically improved.
