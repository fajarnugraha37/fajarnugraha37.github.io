# Part 020 — Platform Maturity Model

## 1. Source notes and scope boundary

This part uses public platform maturity concepts, especially:

- CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model.
- CNCF platform engineering material on internal platforms.
- Team Topologies platform-team and platform-as-a-product ideas.
- Backstage-style developer portal and software catalog concepts as public examples.

This part does **not** assume a specific CSG maturity level, platform team structure, portal tooling, platform roadmap, or golden path implementation.

The purpose is to give you a practical assessment model you can adapt internally.

---

## 2. Core thesis

Platform maturity is not tool maturity.

A company can have Kubernetes, CI/CD, Terraform, service mesh, developer portal, OpenTelemetry, and templates — and still have immature platform engineering.

Maturity means the platform consistently helps product teams deliver safer software with less cognitive load and less coordination friction.

The question is not:

> Do we have a platform?

The question is:

> Does the platform measurably improve the daily flow, safety, and autonomy of product teams?

For enterprise Quote & Order engineering, maturity should be visible in outcomes such as:

- faster local setup,
- faster service creation,
- safer database migrations,
- consistent observability,
- fewer production surprises,
- clearer ownership,
- easier debugging,
- lower support burden,
- higher adoption of paved-road patterns,
- fewer undocumented exceptions.

---

## 3. Maturity dimensions

A useful maturity model should look across multiple dimensions.

This series uses eight practical dimensions:

1. **Investment** — people, time, funding, roadmap, leadership support.
2. **Adoption** — real usage by product teams.
3. **Interfaces** — portal, CLI, APIs, templates, documentation, support channels.
4. **Operations** — reliability, support, incident response, lifecycle ownership.
5. **Measurement** — platform success metrics and feedback loops.
6. **Golden paths** — paved roads for common engineering workflows.
7. **Governance** — standards, policy, exceptions, compliance automation.
8. **Enablement** — onboarding, training, migration support, internal consulting.

A platform can be mature in one dimension and weak in another.

For example:

- strong tooling but weak adoption,
- strong adoption but weak measurement,
- strong standards but poor exception handling,
- strong CI templates but weak observability defaults.

---

## 4. Five maturity levels

Use five simple levels.

| Level | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ad hoc | Teams solve problems independently with scripts and tribal knowledge. |
| 2 | Standardizing | Common templates/processes appear, but adoption is inconsistent. |
| 3 | Self-service | Product teams can use paved roads with limited support. |
| 4 | Productized | Platform is managed as an internal product with roadmap, users, support, metrics. |
| 5 | Adaptive | Platform continuously improves from telemetry, feedback, incidents, and changing needs. |

Maturity is not a trophy.

It is a map for deciding what to improve next.

---

## 5. Level 1 — Ad hoc

### 5.1 Characteristics

- Each team creates its own scripts.
- Setup steps are undocumented or outdated.
- Service ownership is unclear.
- CI/CD patterns differ widely.
- Observability is inconsistent.
- Kubernetes manifests are copied from old services.
- Security and compliance checks are manual or late.
- Production runbooks are missing or tribal.
- New engineers rely heavily on buddy knowledge.

### 5.2 User experience

A developer asks:

- Which repo do I copy from?
- Which service has the latest pattern?
- Why does this environment fail?
- Who owns this dashboard?
- How do I add a Kafka topic?
- How do I debug this service locally?

### 5.3 Common risks

- high cognitive load,
- slow onboarding,
- inconsistent quality,
- duplicated platform work,
- late production readiness issues,
- support dependency on a few people.

### 5.4 Improvement target

Move toward standardization.

Start with:

- service inventory,
- ownership map,
- setup docs,
- common CI template,
- basic logging/health check standard,
- one golden path candidate.

---

## 6. Level 2 — Standardizing

### 6.1 Characteristics

- Some templates exist.
- Some service standards exist.
- CI/CD has common patterns.
- Observability conventions begin to appear.
- Documentation exists but may be stale.
- Adoption depends on team awareness.
- Exceptions are informal.
- Platform support is reactive.

### 6.2 User experience

A developer can find a recommended pattern, but may not know whether it is current.

Questions shift from:

> How do I do this at all?

To:

> Which of these standards is the current one?

### 6.3 Common risks

- standard drift,
- duplicate templates,
- unclear ownership,
- partial adoption,
- platform work still ticket-driven,
- documentation decay.

### 6.4 Improvement target

Move toward self-service.

Invest in:

- versioned templates,
- self-service docs,
- developer portal or catalog,
- clear owner metadata,
- platform support channel,
- adoption tracking,
- exception documentation.

---

## 7. Level 3 — Self-service

### 7.1 Characteristics

- Teams can create or modify services using platform capabilities.
- Golden paths are documented and discoverable.
- CI/CD defaults are reusable.
- Observability defaults are available.
- Service catalog has ownership metadata.
- Common tasks do not require platform tickets.
- Platform team handles harder cases and improvement work.

### 7.2 User experience

A developer can:

- create a service,
- register it in catalog,
- run it locally,
- deploy it,
- observe it,
- follow troubleshooting guide,
- request exception if needed.

### 7.3 Common risks

- self-service works only for happy path,
- support model weak for edge cases,
- platform metrics still shallow,
- product teams use platform but do not give feedback,
- exceptions accumulate silently.

### 7.4 Improvement target

Move toward productization.

Invest in:

- platform roadmap,
- user research,
- adoption analytics,
- lifecycle management,
- release notes,
- SLA/SLO-like expectations for platform capabilities,
- migration plans for old patterns.

---

## 8. Level 4 — Productized

### 8.1 Characteristics

- Platform capabilities have owners.
- Users and jobs-to-be-done are explicit.
- Roadmap is based on product-team friction and outcome metrics.
- Adoption and satisfaction are measured.
- Platform support requests feed roadmap.
- Documentation is maintained as part of product lifecycle.
- Exceptions are governed and reviewed.
- Platform improvements are connected to delivery outcomes.

### 8.2 User experience

Product teams see the platform as an internal product.

They know:

- what it offers,
- how to adopt it,
- how to get help,
- what is supported,
- when changes are coming,
- how to request improvements.

### 8.3 Common risks

- platform success measured by adoption but not outcomes,
- roadmap becomes too broad,
- platform team over-centralizes decisions,
- golden path becomes restrictive,
- product teams lose ownership by delegating too much.

### 8.4 Improvement target

Move toward adaptive improvement.

Invest in:

- continuous telemetry-based improvement,
- friction analytics,
- incident-to-platform feedback,
- proactive deprecation,
- ecosystem contribution,
- platform capability scorecards.

---

## 9. Level 5 — Adaptive

### 9.1 Characteristics

- Platform evolves continuously from telemetry and user feedback.
- Product teams and platform team co-create improvements.
- Platform capabilities are measured by actual reduction in friction/risk.
- Golden paths adapt to new architectures and business needs.
- Obsolete paths are deprecated safely.
- Platform maturity is reviewed periodically.
- Incident and support patterns trigger platform backlog items.
- Governance is mostly automated and embedded.

### 9.2 User experience

Developers experience the platform as:

- discoverable,
- self-service,
- reliable,
- well-supported,
- adaptable,
- useful for real work,
- not a bureaucratic layer.

### 9.3 Common risks

Even mature platforms can fail.

Risks:

- over-automation without human judgment,
- metrics overfitting,
- platform team disconnected from product pain,
- too much abstraction hiding important operational reality,
- inability to support exceptions.

### 9.4 Improvement target

Keep learning.

Maturity is maintained through feedback loops, not achieved once.

## 10. Assessment matrix

Use this matrix to assess each dimension.

| Dimension | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 | Level 4 | Level 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Investment | informal effort | assigned owners | dedicated capacity | roadmap/funding | continuous strategic investment |
| Adoption | local scripts | partial standards | self-service usage | measured adoption | adaptive usage and co-creation |
| Interfaces | tribal knowledge | docs/templates | portal/CLI/API | productized UX | integrated adaptive experience |
| Operations | reactive help | basic support | support channels | support analytics | proactive improvement |
| Measurement | none | output metrics | adoption metrics | outcome metrics | feedback-driven optimization |
| Golden paths | copy/paste | basic templates | self-service paved roads | lifecycle-managed products | continuously evolved defaults |
| Governance | manual | documented standards | automated checks | exception model | adaptive policy-as-code |
| Enablement | buddy-only | docs/training | office hours/migration help | structured enablement | embedded learning loops |

Do not average scores blindly.

Find bottleneck dimensions.

A platform with Level 4 tools and Level 1 adoption is not Level 4 mature.

## 11. Platform maturity by workflow

Assess maturity per workflow, not only globally.

### 11.1 Service creation maturity

Questions:

- Can a team create a new Java service from template?
- Does it include CI/CD, Docker, Kubernetes, health checks, logging, tracing, tests?
- Is service catalog registration automatic?
- Is ownership metadata required?
- Is the path tested regularly?

### 11.2 Local development maturity

Questions:

- Can a new engineer run service locally quickly?
- Are PostgreSQL/Kafka dependencies scripted?
- Are sample fixtures available?
- Are debugging instructions current?
- Is the local path similar enough to production?

### 11.3 Deployment maturity

Questions:

- Is deployment self-service?
- Are rollbacks understood?
- Are config/secrets managed safely?
- Are deployment health checks automatic?
- Is rollout history visible?

### 11.4 Observability maturity

Questions:

- Are logs structured consistently?
- Are traces propagated across services?
- Are dashboards standardized?
- Are business metrics supported?
- Are runbooks linked?

### 11.5 Migration maturity

Questions:

- Are migration templates available?
- Are expand-contract examples documented?
- Are lock risks checked?
- Are Testcontainers migration tests supported?
- Are backfill patterns reusable?

### 11.6 Security maturity

Questions:

- Are secret patterns paved?
- Are vulnerability checks automated?
- Are RBAC patterns reusable?
- Are policy violations visible early?
- Are exceptions tracked?

## 12. Platform maturity for Quote & Order

Quote & Order systems need special maturity signals.

### 12.1 API contract maturity

- OpenAPI generation standardized.
- Contract diff gate available.
- Generated client tests supported.
- TMF mapping documentation discoverable.
- Error model standard available.

### 12.2 Kafka maturity

- Event schema template available.
- Compatibility check integrated.
- Local event fixture support exists.
- DLQ/replay tooling documented.
- Consumer lag dashboards standardized.

### 12.3 PostgreSQL maturity

- Migration dry-run pattern available.
- Query plan diagnostic guide exists.
- Backfill framework/pattern exists.
- Tenant predicate test examples exist.
- Data repair runbook pattern exists.

### 12.4 Observability maturity

- quote ID/order ID correlation supported,
- business metrics for quote/order lifecycle available,
- outbox lag dashboard standardized,
- order fallout dashboard exists,
- alert templates include runbook links.

### 12.5 Release maturity

- feature flag pattern available,
- ORR template available,
- rollback/roll-forward playbooks available,
- on-prem packaging guidance available if relevant,
- customer-specific configuration pattern documented.

## 13. Maturity improvement strategy

Do not try to move everything from Level 1 to Level 5.

Pick high-leverage workflows.

### 13.1 Step 1 — Identify friction

Use:

- developer interviews,
- flow metrics,
- CI data,
- incident history,
- support tickets,
- onboarding observation,
- PR review delays.

### 13.2 Step 2 — Select one workflow

Examples:

- CI feedback loop,
- Java service golden path,
- Kafka event development,
- migration testing,
- observability defaults,
- developer onboarding.

### 13.3 Step 3 — Define current and target maturity

Example:

```md
Workflow: Kafka event development
Current: Level 2 — standardizing
Target: Level 3 — self-service
Evidence: schema examples exist, but teams manually copy patterns and compatibility checks are inconsistent.
Target outcome: teams can add producer/consumer with schema check, local test fixture, and dashboard defaults without platform ticket.
```

### 13.4 Step 4 — Create improvement slice

Do not build a giant platform project.

Create slices:

1. Document current recommended pattern.
2. Add template for one common case.
3. Add CI compatibility check.
4. Add local fixture example.
5. Pilot with one team.
6. Measure adoption and friction.
7. Improve based on feedback.

### 13.5 Step 5 — Measure outcome

Measure:

- adoption,
- time saved,
- error reduction,
- support ticket reduction,
- developer satisfaction,
- delivery flow improvement.

## 15. Maturity anti-patterns

### 15.1 Tool-count maturity

Assumption:

> We have many tools, so we are mature.

Reality:

- maturity is about outcomes and usability.

### 15.2 Portal-as-maturity

Assumption:

> We have a developer portal, so we have a platform.

Reality:

- a portal is an interface, not the whole platform.

### 15.3 Adoption-by-mandate

Assumption:

> Teams use it because policy says so.

Reality:

- forced usage may hide poor DevEx.

### 15.4 Level-chasing

Assumption:

> The goal is Level 5.

Reality:

- the goal is better flow, safety, and autonomy.

### 15.5 Global averaging

Assumption:

> Our platform is Level 3.

Reality:

- service creation may be Level 4 while migration workflow is Level 1.

### 15.6 Maturity without ownership

Assumption:

> The pattern exists, so it is mature.

Reality:

- no owner means it will rot.

## 16. Platform scorecards

A scorecard can help teams see maturity, but it must be helpful rather than punitive.

### 16.1 Useful scorecard categories

- service owner set,
- repository metadata complete,
- CI pipeline healthy,
- tests categorized,
- deployment path documented,
- logs structured,
- traces enabled,
- dashboard exists,
- alert/runbook exists,
- secrets pattern approved,
- dependency scanning enabled,
- API contract published,
- migration pattern compliant.

### 16.2 Scorecard safety rules

- Do not shame teams.
- Show why each item matters.
- Provide remediation path.
- Allow exceptions.
- Track improvement over time.
- Pair scorecard with platform support.

A scorecard without help becomes bureaucracy.

## 17. Internal verification checklist

### 17.1 Organization and ownership

- Is there a platform team?
- Is platform work funded explicitly?
- Are platform capabilities owned?
- Are product teams responsible for production services?
- Are ownership boundaries clear?

### 17.2 Platform capabilities

- Service catalog?
- Developer portal?
- Java service template?
- CI/CD golden path?
- Kubernetes deployment template?
- Observability standard?
- Kafka event standard?
- PostgreSQL migration standard?
- Security/compliance standard?

### 17.3 Adoption and support

- Which teams use each capability?
- Which teams avoid it?
- What support channels exist?
- Are support tickets analyzed?
- Are platform docs trusted?
- Are migrations to golden path supported?

### 17.4 Measurement

- Is platform adoption tracked?
- Is developer satisfaction tracked?
- Is onboarding time tracked?
- Is CI duration tracked?
- Is incident diagnosis time tracked?
- Are platform investments connected to DORA/flow/DevEx outcomes?

### 17.5 Governance and exceptions

- Are standards documented?
- Are policy checks automated?
- Are exceptions tracked?
- Do exceptions expire?
- Are recurring exceptions used to improve the platform?

---

## 18. Senior engineer review questions

When looking at platform maturity, ask:

- Which workflow hurts product teams most today?
- Which maturity dimension blocks improvement?
- Is the problem lack of tools, lack of adoption, lack of support, or lack of measurement?
- Is the golden path actually easier than custom work?
- Are teams avoiding the platform for valid reasons?
- Are platform standards reducing or increasing cognitive load?
- Do platform metrics show outcome improvement?
- Is there an exception process?
- Is there a deprecation process?
- Does the platform help production ownership or obscure it?

## 20. Summary

Platform maturity means product teams can move safely and quickly with less cognitive load.

A mature platform has:

- investment,
- adoption,
- usable interfaces,
- operational ownership,
- meaningful measurement,
- golden paths,
- governance without bureaucracy,
- enablement.

For enterprise teams, maturity must be assessed per workflow:

- service creation,
- local development,
- CI/CD,
- observability,
- migration,
- security,
- Kafka,
- PostgreSQL,
- Kubernetes.

The senior engineer's job is not to chase maturity labels.

The job is to identify the platform capability that most improves product-team flow, safety, and autonomy — then help move it one maturity level forward.
